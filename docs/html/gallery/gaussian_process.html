A Gaussian-Process Model¶

This page replaces the neural network with a Gaussian process, a model that reports its own confidence alongside every prediction. It learns from real measured data, and hybrax trains it the same way it trains every other model in this gallery.

This page is inspired by two papers. Helleckes et al. 2024 [1] fit a Gaussian process to real bioprocess measurements to predict reaction rates. Cruz-Bournazou et al. 2022 [2] showed that training a Gaussian process on a smooth curve’s derivative works better than using the raw difference between two measurements. This page builds a Gaussian process reaction module that combines both ideas. See How This Compares to the Papers at the end of this page for exactly where it matches each paper and where it departs.

The walkthrough below shows the file in pieces, next to the reasoning for each one.

The Kernel¶

A squared-exponential kernel with automatic relevance determination (ARD): one lengthscale per input feature, so the module can learn that some state axes matter more than others to “how similar are these two states.”

 1class GPReactionModule(UserReactionModule):
 2    centers: jax.Array = frozen_field()  # Z, real states, (n_points, n_features)
 3    targets: jax.Array = frozen_field()  # y, real rate estimates, (n_points, n_rates)
 4    log_lengthscale: jax.Array = trainable_field()  # (n_features,), ARD
 5    log_output_scale: jax.Array = trainable_field()  # scalar kernel amplitude
 6    log_noise: jax.Array = trainable_field()  # scalar jitter
 7
 8    def __init__(self, *, centers, targets, **scale_kwargs):
 9        super().__init__(**scale_kwargs)
10        n_features = self.n_modeled_RMCs
11        self.centers = centers
12        self.targets = targets
13        self.log_lengthscale = jnp.zeros(n_features)
14        self.log_output_scale = jnp.array(0.0)
15        self.log_noise = jnp.array(-2.0)
16
17    def _kernel(self, a, b):
18        diff = (a[:, None, :] - b[None, :, :]) / jnp.exp(self.log_lengthscale)
19        sq_dist = jnp.sum(diff**2, axis=-1)
20        return jnp.exp(self.log_output_scale) * jnp.exp(-0.5 * sq_dist)

centers are real states (Z in the usual GP notation), one per real measurement, built by build_reaction_module below. targets are the real rate estimates at those same states (y). Both are frozen_field(): real data, held fixed through training. log_lengthscale, log_output_scale, and log_noise stay trainable_field(): they are what training actually fits.

The Posterior¶

 1    def _chol(self):
 2        k_zz = self._kernel(self.centers, self.centers)
 3        k_zz = k_zz + jnp.exp(self.log_noise) * jnp.eye(self.centers.shape[0])
 4        return jsl.cho_factor(k_zz, lower=True)
 5
 6    def __call__(self, t, inputs: ReactionInputs) -> ReactionOutputs:
 7        del t
 8        x = inputs.SCL_modeled_RMCs[None, :]
 9        chol = self._chol()
10        k_xz = self._kernel(x, self.centers)
11        mean = (k_xz @ jsl.cho_solve(chol, self.targets))[0]
12        v = jsl.cho_solve(chol, k_xz[0])
13        var = jnp.exp(self.log_output_scale) - k_xz[0] @ v
14        SCL_rate_std = jnp.sqrt(jnp.clip(var, 1e-12)) * jnp.ones_like(mean)
15        # predictions.csv reports q_* in RAW units (RAW_modeled_BiologicalOde_rates),
16        # but auxiliary values pass through unscaled: convert here so rate_std is
17        # comparable to q_* in the same file, not left in SCL units next to RAW ones.
18        RAW_rate_std = self.unscale_modeled_BiologicalOde_rates(SCL_rate_std)
19        return ReactionOutputs(
20            SCL_modeled_BiologicalOde_rates=mean,
21            SCL_modeled_Outflows_rates=jnp.zeros(0),
22            SCL_modeled_Inflows_rates=jnp.zeros(0),
23            auxiliary={"rate_std": RAW_rate_std},
24        )

jax.scipy.linalg.cho_factor/cho_solve over the real, full kernel matrix give a genuine closed-form GP posterior, over the model’s real training data: mean is the predictive mean (what gets returned as the rate), var is the predictive variance at the current state.

The predictive std goes straight into auxiliary, which hybrax.train threads into predictions.csv as extra columns: no new plumbing needed.

estimate_all_scales is unchanged from Tutorial 4.

The Training Data¶

 1def build_reaction_module(*, seed, training_parent_collection, **kwargs):
 2    del seed
 3    scale_kwargs = {k: v for k, v in kwargs.items() if k.startswith("SCALE_")}
 4    first_process = next(iter(training_parent_collection.processes.values()))
 5    rhs = build_rhs_ode(first_process)
 6    rmc_names = list(rhs.name_modeled_RMCs)
 7    rmc_scaler = scale_kwargs["SCALE_modeled_RMCs"]
 8    rate_scaler = scale_kwargs["SCALE_modeled_BiologicalOde_rates"]
 9
10    centers_list = []
11    targets_list = []
12    for process in training_parent_collection.processes.values():
13        process.pseudobatch_transform = build_pseudobatch_transform(process)
14        meas_times = np.asarray(
15            process.reactor_medium.components["biomass"].concentration.times
16        )
17        splines = {name: build_backtransform_spline(process, name) for name in rmc_names}
18        values = {name: np.asarray(splines[name](meas_times)) for name in rmc_names}
19        derivatives = {
20            name: np.asarray(splines[name].derivative()(meas_times)) for name in rmc_names
21        }
22        biomass = values["biomass"]
23
24        # q_biomass/q_glucose/q_product are all specific rates: the declared
25        # ODE is "<species>' = q_<species> * biomass" for every one of them.
26        raw_state = np.stack([values[name] for name in rmc_names], axis=1)
27        raw_rate = np.stack([derivatives[name] / biomass for name in rmc_names], axis=1)
28
29        centers_list.append(np.asarray(rmc_scaler.scale_value(jnp.asarray(raw_state))))
30        # A rate is a derivative, not a value: scale_derivative, not
31        # scale_value, so an affine scaler's offset (if any) is never
32        # subtracted from it.
33        targets_list.append(
34            np.asarray(rate_scaler.scale_derivative(jnp.asarray(raw_rate)))
35        )
36
37    centers = jnp.asarray(np.concatenate(centers_list, axis=0))
38    targets = jnp.asarray(np.concatenate(targets_list, axis=0))
39
40    return GPReactionModule(centers=centers, targets=targets, **scale_kwargs)

For every process, hybrax.format.splines.build_pseudobatch_transform() and hybrax.format.splines.build_backtransform_spline() fit a smooth spline through the real measured concentrations. Calling .derivative() on that spline gives the real dc/dt at every real measurement time. Dividing by the real biomass value at that time turns it into a specific rate, matching the declared ODE: biomass' = q_biomass * biomass.

This is Cruz-Bournazou et al. 2022’s core idea [2]: a smooth curve’s derivative is a better rate estimate than a raw finite difference between two points. The pseudobatch spline is the general form of that curve: it fits the dilution-corrected concentration first, so a bolus or sampling event’s jump never distorts the fit. This gallery’s data has no such event, but the same code handles one correctly either way.

rmc_scaler.scale_value converts a real state to SCL space. A rate needs the derivative-specific method instead, rate_scaler.scale_derivative: subtracting a value-space offset from a derivative would silently corrupt it.

Fitting the Kernel by Marginal Likelihood¶

 1    def marginal_nll(self) -> jax.Array:
 2        """Real GP negative log marginal likelihood over centers/targets.
 3
 4        Fits ``log_lengthscale``/``log_output_scale``/``log_noise`` the way a
 5        textbook GP does: maximizing the probability of the real training
 6        pairs, not the downstream trajectory.
 7        """
 8        chol = self._chol()
 9        alpha = jsl.cho_solve(chol, self.targets)  # (n_points, n_rates)
10        n_points, n_rates = self.targets.shape
11        data_fit = 0.5 * jnp.sum(self.targets * alpha)
12        log_det = n_rates * jnp.sum(jnp.log(jnp.diagonal(chol[0])))
13        n_terms = n_points * n_rates
14        return (data_fit + log_det + 0.5 * n_terms * jnp.log(2 * jnp.pi)) / n_terms

marginal_nll is the standard GP negative log marginal likelihood, computed from the module’s own real centers and targets. It’s the quantity a textbook GP fit maximizes, reusing the same Cholesky factor __call__ already computes.

 1class GPLossModule(DefaultLossModule):
 2    """The usual per-target trajectory loss, plus the GP's own marginal
 3    likelihood: both drive the same `hybrax.train` gradient step.
 4    """
 5
 6    nll_weight: float = eqx.field(static=True)
 7
 8    def __init__(self, *, target_names, nll_weight=0.0005):
 9        super().__init__(target_names=target_names)
10        self.nll_weight = nll_weight
11
12    @property
13    def loss_names(self):
14        return (*self.target_names, "gp_nll")
15
16    def __call__(self, inputs: LossInputs) -> LossOutputs:
17        base = super().__call__(inputs)
18        gp_nll = inputs.reaction_module.marginal_nll()
19        return LossOutputs(
20            named_losses={**base.named_losses, "gp_nll": self.nll_weight * gp_nll}
21        )

GPLossModule adds one more named loss, gp_nll, to the usual per-target trajectory MSE terms DefaultLossModule already provides. inputs.reaction_module is the live reaction-module instance, so marginal_nll() is reachable directly from inside the loss. Both terms drive the same gradient step: the trajectory loss keeps the whole model anchored to the real measured concentrations, and gp_nll fits the kernel hyperparameters the way a real GP does. nll_weight scales gp_nll down to keep it in the same rough range as the trajectory terms, since hybrax.train averages named losses and a much larger term would dominate the average.

What the GP Actually Learned¶

centers: spread (std) per feature = [0.33065791 0.35226018 0.32698568]
lengthscale (exp)   = [0.50718269 0.58452231 0.29145484]
output_scale (exp)  = 7.326
noise (exp)         = 1.764

centers’ spread reflects the real training data: a frozen_field() stays fixed through training. Lengthscales near the same order of magnitude as the state itself (roughly 1, since states are in SCL space) mean the kernel uses genuine spatial structure. A lengthscale near zero means the kernel is memorizing individual points; a huge lengthscale means it is ignoring that input entirely. Those lengthscale, output-scale, and noise values are what gp_nll actually fit.

gp_nll: first = 0.0940, last = 0.0077

gp_nll falling over training shows the marginal-likelihood term actually moving the kernel hyperparameters toward a better fit to the real training pairs.

Reading Out the Uncertainty¶

aux_rate_std_0/1/2 are ReactionOutputs.auxiliary["rate_std"], one column per rate, written out with no extra code on top of what every other page already does. All three bands are identical width at a given timestep, since the kernel is shared across every rate output and only targets differs between them. That is a real simplification: an independent kernel per rate is a natural next step, left for later.

The band widens somewhat around the middle of the trajectory and narrows again toward the end. That shape comes directly from the kernel: rate_std grows where run_1’s own state is farther from the pooled real training states across all three runs, and shrinks where it sits close to them.

Gotchas¶

  • The kernel is shared across all rate outputs. rate_std is one number per timestep, shared by every rate: read it as “how confident is the model here,” never as confidence about one specific rate.

  • Two objectives fit the kernel hyperparameters together. gp_nll and the trajectory loss both move log_lengthscale, log_output_scale, and log_noise every step. A textbook GP maximizes marginal likelihood alone; the fitted values here are a compromise between the two, weighted by nll_weight.

  • nll_weight needs tuning if you change the data or the epoch budget. hybrax.train averages named losses, so an unscaled gp_nll can either get drowned out by the per-target MSE terms or dominate them. Watch gp_nll and the per-target losses together in metrics.csv: both should fall steadily, together, through training.

How This Compares to the Papers¶

The kernel, the real training data, and the marginal-likelihood objective for the kernel hyperparameters all match Helleckes et al. 2024’s paper [1]. Their kernel hyperparameters are fit by likelihood alone, once, then held fixed for prediction. Here they are fit by likelihood and trajectory error together, continuously, so the fitted values differ from what a pure-likelihood fit would find. See Knowledge Transfer for the paper’s other headline result, pooling data across products, reproduced the same way.

Cruz-Bournazou et al. 2022’s core idea [2], training a GP on a smooth curve’s derivative instead of a raw difference between two points, builds the real rate targets above. Their further step, iteratively refitting that curve against the GP’s own predictions, stays out of scope here: the trajectory loss plays a comparable role, through real continuous integration.
